A. Genetic Algorithms

1. Motivation and Basic Idea

Genetic Algorithms (GAs) are learning and search methods motivated by biological evolution. They maintain a population of candidate hypotheses. In each generation, relatively fit hypotheses are more likely to contribute offspring. New hypotheses are created by recombining parts of parent hypotheses and by randomly mutating some parts.

The population is updated repeatedly. Some members may survive directly, while others are replaced by offspring. The process continues until an acceptable fitness threshold is reached or another stopping condition is satisfied.

GAs are useful when the hypothesis space is large and contains complex interacting parts. They do not require a simple mathematical description of how each component affects overall fitness. The slides also highlight that GAs can be parallelized.

2. Main Components and Algorithm

The slide deck identifies four central parts: representing hypotheses, genetic operators, fitness function and selection. The algorithm also requires a population size, replacement fraction, mutation rate and acceptable fitness threshold.

  1. Generate an initial population of candidate hypotheses.
  2. Evaluate each candidate using the fitness function.
  3. Check whether the best candidate reaches the acceptable fitness threshold.
  4. Select members probabilistically for survival or reproduction.
  5. Generate offspring using crossover.
  6. Apply random mutation to a chosen fraction of the new population.
  7. Form the next generation and repeat.
Stopping condition: A GA may stop when the fitness threshold is met. In practical implementations, a maximum number of generations or lack of improvement may also be used; these are general implementation choices, not additional details specified in the slides.

3. Representing Hypotheses

Hypotheses in GAs are often represented as bit strings because bit strings can be manipulated easily by crossover and mutation. A chromosome is an encoded candidate solution.

The slides use the attribute Outlook, which can take three values: Sunny, Overcast and Rain. A three-bit substring can represent which values are allowed:

Bit stringInterpretation
010Outlook = Overcast only
011Outlook = Overcast OR Rain; a more general constraint
111Any Outlook value is allowed; “don't care” / most general constraint

In this representation, a 1 means that the corresponding attribute value is permitted, while a 0 means it is excluded. Changing a 0 to 1 relaxes a constraint and makes the hypothesis more general.

Classroom check: If the bit order is Sunny, Overcast, Rain, what does 101 mean? It permits Sunny or Rain and excludes Overcast.

4. Genetic Operators

4.1 Crossover

Crossover creates offspring by copying selected bit positions from two parent strings. The slides describe a crossover mask that determines which parent contributes each bit position.

Worked crossover example

Parent 1: 110010
Parent 2: 001101
Crossover mask: 101010

Use Parent 1 where the mask is 1 and Parent 2 where it is 0:

Offspring 1: 100000

The complementary choice gives Offspring 2: 011111.

This demonstrates mask-based bit selection. Different mask conventions may label the two complementary offspring in the reverse order.

4.2 Mutation

Mutation randomly alters selected bits in a chromosome. For a binary representation, a common mutation is bit flipping: 0 becomes 1 or 1 becomes 0. Mutation introduces new variations that may not be present in the current population.

Example: Chromosome 101100. If the fourth bit mutates, the new chromosome becomes 101000.

Crossover recombines existing information from parents; mutation introduces random changes. Both help explore the hypothesis space.

5. Fitness Function and Selection

The fitness function ranks candidate hypotheses. In the slide deck's prototype GA, fitness-proportionate selection assigns a candidate a probability proportional to its fitness.

\[P(h_i\text{ selected})=\frac{Fitness(h_i)}{\sum_j Fitness(h_j)}\]

This is also called roulette-wheel selection. A candidate with higher fitness receives a larger probability of selection, but a lower-fitness candidate may still be selected.

Worked numerical example – roulette-wheel selection

Suppose a population contains four hypotheses with fitness values 10, 20, 30 and 40. Total fitness = 100.

HypothesisFitnessSelection probability
H11010/100 = 0.10 (10%)
H22020/100 = 0.20 (20%)
H33030/100 = 0.30 (30%)
H44040/100 = 0.40 (40%)

H4 is most likely to be selected, but selection is probabilistic rather than guaranteed.

Other selection methods

  • Tournament selection: choose two hypotheses randomly. With probability \(p\), select the fitter one; with probability \(1-p\), select the less fit one. The slides note that tournament selection can preserve more population diversity than roulette-wheel selection.
  • Rank selection: sort candidates by fitness and assign selection probability according to rank rather than raw fitness.

7. Hypothesis-Space Search, Crowding and Schemas

GA search can move abruptly through the hypothesis space: an offspring may differ substantially from its parent. The slides identify crowding as a practical difficulty.

Crowding occurs when a highly fit individual reproduces rapidly, causing many copies of it and very similar individuals to occupy a large part of the population. This reduces diversity and can slow further progress because fewer distinct regions of the search space are explored.

Methods for reducing crowding (as listed in the slides)

  • Change the selection function, for example use tournament or rank selection instead of roulette-wheel selection.
  • Use fitness sharing.
  • Restrict which individuals are allowed to recombine to form offspring.

Schemas and schema theorem

Schema analysis studies how such patterns may increase or decrease in a population over generations. This explanatory definition is background clarification; the uploaded slides do not show the full formal derivation.

8. Genetic Programming

Genetic programming evolves programs or symbolic expressions rather than fixed-length bit strings. The slide deck introduces a block-stacking problem in which the goal is to produce the word UNIVERSAL.

The slide lists three terminals:

  • CS (Current Stack): name of the top block on the stack, or F.
  • TB (Top Correct Block): name of the topmost correct block.
  • NN (Next Necessary): name of the next block needed above TB in the stack.

The slides report that a population of 300 programs was trained on 166 test problems and found the displayed program after 10 generations:

\[\texttt{(EQ (DU (MT CS)(NOT CS)) (DU (MS NN)(NOT NN)))}\]

The expression is reproduced as shown in the slide. The slides do not define every function symbol (EQ, DU, MT, MS) in the extracted text, so a precise operational interpretation is not supplied here.

9. Models of Evolution and Learning

9.1 Lamarckian evolution

The slides describe Lamarck's proposal that experiences acquired during an organism's lifetime could directly affect the genetic makeup passed to offspring. The example given is an organism learning to avoid toxic food and supposedly passing that acquired trait genetically to its offspring.

The slides contrast this with the currently accepted view that an individual's genetic makeup is not changed directly by the lifetime experience of its biological parents.

9.2 Baldwin effect

The Baldwin effect concerns the interaction between individual learning and evolution across generations. The slide deck describes the Hinton and Nowlan (1987) experiment using simple neural networks. Some network weights were fixed during an individual's lifetime while others were trainable; the genetic makeup determined which weights were trainable.

When individual learning was not allowed, the population failed to improve its fitness over time. When individual learning was allowed, the population quickly improved its fitness. The classroom point is that learning during an individual's lifetime can make useful behaviours achievable and thereby influence which inherited configurations are favoured over generations, without requiring acquired traits themselves to be directly inherited.

B. Combining Inductive and Analytical Learning

11. Motivation

Machine learning includes two broad paradigms:

Inductive learningAnalytical learning
Generalizes from specific examples or data points.Uses logical inference and prior domain knowledge.
Builds hypotheses by detecting patterns in observed data.Builds or refines hypotheses by deductive reasoning.
Examples in the slides: decision-tree induction and neural-network backpropagation.Example in the slides: PROLOG-EBG / explanation-based learning.
Strength: works well with plentiful data and can adapt to unseen examples.Strength: can learn from scarce data when prior knowledge is strong.
Limitation: needs sufficient reliable labelled data; may overfit.Limitation: depends heavily on the correctness and availability of prior knowledge.

Inductive methods provide statistical justification, while analytical methods provide logical justification. Statistical methods may be weak when data is scarce; analytical methods may be misleading when prior knowledge is incorrect or unavailable. Combining them aims to use the strengths of both.

12. The Learning Problem

The slide deck defines a combined learning problem using three inputs:

  • A set of training examples \(D\), possibly containing errors.
  • A domain theory \(B\), possibly containing errors.
  • A space of candidate hypotheses \(H\).

The goal is to find a hypothesis that fits both the training examples and the domain theory as well as possible.

The training-data error is the proportion of examples from \(D\) misclassified by \(h\):

\[\operatorname{error}_{D}(h)=\text{proportion of examples in }D\text{ misclassified by }h\]

The domain-theory error is the probability that \(h\) disagrees with \(B\) on the classification of a randomly drawn instance:

\[\operatorname{error}_{B}(h)=\Pr[h(x)\ne B(x)]\]

The slides propose minimizing a combined measure such as:

\[\boxed{h^*=\arg\min_{h\in H}\left[k_D\operatorname{error}_{D}(h)+k_B\operatorname{error}_{B}(h)\right]}\]

Here \(k_D\) and \(k_B\) control the relative importance assigned to data and domain theory. The slides point out that it is not obvious how to choose these weights in advance.

Reasoning through the weights
  • If the domain theory is poor but the training data is plentiful and reliable, place greater importance on \(\operatorname{error}_D(h)\).
  • If the domain theory is strong but the available data is small and noisy, place greater importance on \(\operatorname{error}_B(h)\).

The slides discuss a Bayesian perspective: priors and likelihood information can be combined with observed data to obtain a posterior probability for a hypothesis. However, Bayes' theorem alone does not solve the general problem if the relevant probability distributions are themselves imperfectly known.

14. Using Prior Knowledge to Initialize the Hypothesis – KBANN

KBANN stands for Knowledge-Based Artificial Neural Network. It uses a domain theory to construct an initial neural network. Backpropagation then refines the network using the training examples.

Two stages

  1. Analytical initialization: translate the domain theory, represented as propositional non-recursive Horn clauses, into an equivalent neural network.
  2. Inductive refinement: apply backpropagation to adjust network weights so that the network fits the training examples.

A Horn clause in this setting is propositional if it contains no variables. The slides assume a domain theory consisting of propositional, non-recursive Horn clauses.

How a Horn clause becomes a network unit

The slides describe creating a sigmoid threshold unit for each Horn clause. A sigmoid output greater than 0.5 is interpreted as True; an output below 0.5 is interpreted as False. Inputs correspond to the antecedents of the clause.

  • For a non-negated antecedent, assign a positive weight \(+W\).
  • For a negated antecedent, assign a negative weight \(-W\).
  • Set the threshold/bias so the unit behaves like a logical AND of its antecedents.
  • Add near-zero additional connections between network units at successive depths, allowing later refinement.

The slide's displayed bias convention is \(w_0=-(n-0.5)W\), where \(n\) is the number of non-negated antecedents. With a sufficiently large \(W\), the sigmoid unit can encode the clause's logical condition.

Simple logical illustration

Consider the rule:

\[\text{Cup}\leftarrow\text{Stable}\land\text{Liftable}\land\text{OpenVessel}\]

The network can represent Stable, Liftable and OpenVessel as inputs to a unit that activates when all three conditions are satisfied. The output unit represents Cup.

The Cup example in the slides

The domain theory in the slide deck decomposes the concept Cup into intermediate properties:

  • Stable ← BottomIsFlat
  • Liftable ← Graspable AND Light
  • Graspable ← HasHandle
  • OpenVessel ← HasConcavity AND ConcavityPointsUp
  • Cup ← Stable AND Liftable AND OpenVessel

The examples describe physical objects using properties such as material, weight, handle, concavity and bottom shape. The initial network reproduces the domain theory. Because the domain theory and training examples are not perfectly consistent, backpropagation changes weights during refinement. The slides state that the refined network correctly classifies the displayed training examples.

Remarks and limitations

  • KBANN uses domain-specific theory to bias generalization.
  • Backpropagation also has a domain-independent syntactic bias toward small weight values.
  • KBANN, as described in the slides, accommodates propositional domain theories consisting of variable-free Horn clauses.
  • Highly inaccurate domain theories can mislead KBANN; its generalization accuracy may become worse than ordinary backpropagation.

15. Using Prior Knowledge to Alter the Search Objective

15.1 TANGENTPROP

Ordinary neural-network training examples are pairs \((x_i,f(x_i))\), containing an input and its target value. TANGENTPROP additionally assumes that training derivatives of the target function are available.

\[\left(x_i,\ f(x_i),\ \left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right|_{x_i}\right)\]

These derivatives provide information about how the target function should change near a training point. Fitting both target values and derivatives can improve generalization when training data is sparse.

The slide deck explains that backpropagation normally favours a smooth interpolation between observed points. Derivative information can override that usual inductive bias by explicitly specifying required changes in the function.

For handwritten-character recognition, the slides give an example of a transformation \(s(\alpha,x_i)\) and a derivative constraint:

\[\left.\frac{\partial f(s(\alpha,x_i))}{\partial\alpha}\right|_{\alpha=0}=0\]

This expresses that the output should remain unchanged for the specified transformation, such as a small transformation of the input image. The general TANGENTPROP method adds a penalty for disagreement between the required training derivatives and the derivatives of the learned network.

Source limitation: The exact expanded TANGENTPROP error-function equation is shown as an image in the slides but is not legible in the extracted text. The notes therefore explain its components without inventing the missing full equation.

Remarks

The slides caution that TANGENTPROP is not robust to incorrect prior derivative information. If the supplied derivative constraints are wrong, the learner may fit incorrect derivatives and generalize less accurately than ordinary backpropagation.

15.2 EBNN – Explanation-Based Neural Network Learning

EBNN combines analytical explanations and neural-network learning in two important ways:

  1. Instead of requiring the user to supply training derivatives, EBNN computes them for each observed training example. It explains the example using the domain theory and extracts derivative information from the explanation.
  2. It addresses how to weight the inductive and analytical contributions. The weighting is chosen independently for each training example using a heuristic that considers how accurately the domain theory predicts that example's target value.

Thus, when the domain theory explains an example well, its analytical guidance can receive greater influence; when the theory predicts poorly, the data-driven component can receive greater influence.

Source limitation: The slides include the heading and an error-function image for EBNN, but the equation symbols and full formula are not reliably readable in the supplied file. The method is therefore described using the legible explanatory text only.

16. Using Prior Knowledge to Augment Search Operators – FOCL

FOCL (First Order Combined Learner) extends the purely inductive FOIL (First-Order Inductive Learner) system. Both learn sets of first-order Horn clauses to cover observed training examples.

Both methods use a general-to-specific search, beginning with the most general possible Horn clause and adding conditions to specialize it.

FOILFOCL
Uses inductive search operators.Uses inductive operators plus alternatives derived from domain theory.
At each step, adds a single new literal to revise the current clause.Can add multiple literals in a single search step when justified by domain theory.
Search proceeds through a sequence of smaller specializations.Can make larger “macro-moves” through the hypothesis space.

These macro-moves can dramatically alter the search path. Consequently, the final hypothesis found by FOCL may differ from the hypothesis found by using only FOIL's original inductive search steps.

Illustrative idea

Suppose a rule learner needs to add three related conditions before a candidate rule becomes useful. A purely inductive learner may add one literal at a time. If the domain theory supports the relationship among all three conditions, FOCL can use that knowledge to introduce a multi-literal move in one step.
